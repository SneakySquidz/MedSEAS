What drives salt

The sea loses more water to evaporation than rain and rivers return, and the water left behind gets saltier. Evaporation is estimated per side of the sea, so the eastern basins lose more (0.55 m/yr, estimate) than the western ones (0.32 m/yr, estimate).

    How it works: a connected-threshold model on today’s terrain at about 4 km per cell. A cell floods only when the sea can reach it over lower ground (priority flood), so inland hollows stay dry until the rim is overtopped. Below −130 m the world ocean is held at −130 m and only the Mediterranean is lowered. Not modelled: coastal defences, drainage, tides, waves, ice sheets, sediment and crustal rebound. A city counts as reached when the water reaches any land cell within one cell (about 4 km) of it; a city whose surrounding cells are all sea on this grid counts as reached at the first rise.     Where the data comes from
    Reading the numbers: seasonal means, surface salinity, and how the Calypso Deep depth was measured.

    Summer (July–September) and winter (January–March) SST are 1991–2020 climatological means of the Copernicus reanalysis over each basin’s 0.25° cells, weighted by cos(latitude): open-water averages, not coastal extremes, so shallow coastal corners can run warmer in August. Salinity is surface salinity in practical salinity units (≈ g/kg); the Aegean mean hides a steep gradient, from brackish Black Sea outflow in the north (below 33) to over 39 in the Cretan Sea. The Calypso Deep figure is the 2020 direct measurement of 5,109 m ±1 m from the submersible Limiting Factor, superseding older 5,121–5,267 m sonar estimates; the litter found there was logged in a separate 2025 study of video filmed on that same dive. The map's “Balearic Sea” follows the broad sense of that name — Britannica treats it as a synonym for the Algerian/Algéro-Provençal Basin, the whole open basin between Algeria, France and the Balearic Islands, not just the strip around Mallorca — so its area is the sourced ~0.85 million km² western-basin total minus the separately-mapped Alboran, Ligurian and Tyrrhenian. Basin areas otherwise vary with where you draw the boundaries; all are given to the nearest thousand km².

    Estimates marked est.: how warming rate and net evaporation were derived, and where they stop.

    are interpolated across a documented gradient rather than measured basin by basin. Warming rate: in the multi-decade satellite SST record, the Balearic, Tyrrhenian, Adriatic and northern Ionian trend fastest at 0.4–0.5 °C/decade, while the Aegean and northern Levantine trend highest basin-wide, up to 0.6 °C/decade (Copernicus Ocean State Report); the Alboran and Ligurian sit below that band. These are long-run averages — Argo float data for 2012–2025 alone show still steeper warming across the whole basin (up to 0.8–0.9 °C/decade near the surface in the western Mediterranean and Ionian), reflecting how much faster the sea has warmed in just the last decade. Net evaporation is evaporation minus rainfall, and each sub-basin carries its parent basin’s average: 0.88 mm/day (≈0.32 m a year) over the western Mediterranean and 1.52 mm/day (≈0.55 m a year) over the eastern, where drought since the late 1990s has intensified it. Comparable per-sub-basin figures are not published; the Adriatic in particular is so diluted by rivers that its real freshwater balance is far less negative than the eastern average suggests. Rivers replace only about a third of what the sea loses to evaporation — the Atlantic inflow at Gibraltar makes up the deficit, and that imbalance is why the sea gets saltier from west to east. Plankton layer: the literature reports the deep chlorophyll maximum at about 30 m in the westernmost Mediterranean (Dolan et al. 2002, as cited in a 2015 Biogeosciences Discussions paper, bgd-12-4139-2015) and at more than 100 m in the Levantine; the per-basin values here, including 120 m for the easternmost Levantine, are placed along that west-to-east gradient and are not measured basin by basin. Posidonia meadows is a five-step presence rating, not an area: Posidonia oceanica is endemic to the Mediterranean and 1,224,707 ha of it have been mapped basin-wide (Telesca et al. 2015, Scientific Reports 5, 12505), but per-basin areas are not published on a comparable footing. The ratings follow the documented range limits — the Alboran is the species' western edge, meadows are rare or absent in the low-salinity northern Adriatic, and they stop at a sharp border in the Levantine, where records from the Syrian, Lebanese and Israeli coasts are now considered erroneous. Meadows are estimated to have contracted by 34% over the last fifty years (same study). Biggest tide is the largest spring range observed anywhere in the basin, not a basin-wide average — the Ionian figure is the Gulf of Gabès, the Adriatic figure is the head of the gulf at Venice, and most of the sea sits nearer 0.2–0.3 m.

    Basin outlines and depths: how the boundaries and terrain were drawn, and what the sea-level scenarios leave out.

    follow the conventional hydrographic divisions at the sills — Gibraltar, the Sicily Channel, Otranto, the Dardanelles and the Cretan Arc — set as straight lines between capes, the convention the IHO uses, then clipped to the Natural Earth 1:10m coastline, simplified. The map also shows the surrounding seas (Atlantic, Black Sea, Sea of Marmara, Red Sea) in grey, outside the eight basins. Area and mean depth are measured from the outlined polygons and the terrain grid, so they describe what is drawn; they can differ from published figures for the same basin names, which use other limits. Maximum depths are literature values. The terrain grid is the AWS/Mapzen “terrarium” terrain tiles (SRTM, GMTED and ETOPO1/GEBCO), averaged to about 4 km per cell, so depths under the cursor are approximate. Sea-level scenarios: an educational scenario on today’s terrain — no ice-sheet or crustal rebound, no erosion or sediment, no tides, waves, defences or drainage — and whether water reaches a city is judged on 4 km cells (see the method note beside the tool). The map is thematic, not navigational. Compiled September 2026.
